# Content from real work

_Generated from the course source. Do not edit this file by hand._

Outcome: A brand voice file and a first piece written from work your experts actually did. About 35 minutes.

This module is optional. Use it only when the person has said that marketing, content or being found online matters to them.

The idea is simple. Content that reads like everyone else's does little. Content written from work the company's own experts actually did, in a consistent voice, gives readers something they cannot get elsewhere. Claude's part is to hold the voice and do the drafting. The substance has to come from the experts and from real jobs.

Starting is easy: a voice file and a first piece. Publishing straight from Claude to the website is harder, and the last step says where that starts.

Do not promise results. Do not state or estimate traffic, leads or revenue from content.

## content.voice: Write the brand voice file from examples you are happy with

### Ask

1. "Paste three to five pieces of writing from the company that you are happy with. A web page, an email, a post. Remove names if they are not public."
2. "Paste one piece you are not happy with, and tell me why."
3. "Who is the reader? Describe the person, not the market."
4. "Which words or phrases do you never want to see?"
5. "Which spelling, formats and names are fixed?" If a company context file exists, reuse its answers.

### Do

Work out the voice from the examples, not from adjectives. Look at sentence length, how the pieces open and close, how technical they get, how they address the reader, what they leave out. Then write the file and show it.

```markdown
# Brand voice: [Company name]

Last reviewed: [date] by [role].

## Who we write for
[One or two sentences about the reader and what they want from us.]

## How we sound
- [Trait, with what it means in practice. For example: Direct. We give the answer in the first sentence.]
- [Trait, with what it means in practice.]
- [Trait, with what it means in practice.]

## We write like this
> [Two or three short passages from the approved examples.]

## We do not write like this
> [A passage from the rejected example, or a made-up one in that style.] Why not: [reason].

## Words
- We say: [terms]. We do not say: [terms].
- Never: [banned phrases].

## Rules
- Every claim comes from our own work or a named source. No invented figures.
- No client is named without their written permission.
- Formats: [spelling, dates, company name].
- [Length and structure for the usual formats: article, post, email.]
```

Test it once: rewrite one paragraph of the rejected example using the file. Ask whether it now sounds like the company. Adjust the file, not the paragraph.

Save it where the team keeps shared files. If the company has a content project in Claude, add it there. If a skill is wanted later, this file is its main reference.

### Done when

- The voice file is written from the person's own examples and they have approved it.
- One rewritten paragraph was accepted as sounding right.
- The file is saved where the people who write can reach it.

### Mark blocked when

- The person has no writing they are happy with. Ask who in the company writes best and mark the step `blocked` until examples exist.
- The voice needs approval from a brand owner who is not available.

### Summary to report

State that the file exists and what it was built from. Do not quote it. Example: "Brand voice file written from four approved examples and one rejected one: three traits, a word list and five rules. Test paragraph accepted after one change."

## content.sources: List the real work and the experts that content should come from

### Ask

1. "Which jobs or projects from the last few months are you proud of, or taught you something?"
2. "What questions do customers ask again and again before they buy or while you work for them?"
3. "For each: which expert in the company did the work or knows the answer best? Role only."
4. "Which of these can be talked about publicly, and which need the client's permission or must stay anonymous?"
5. "How much time can each expert give: twenty minutes of talking is enough for one piece."

### Do

Build the source list.

| Real job or question | What a reader would learn from it | Expert (role) | Can it be public? (yes / anonymised / needs permission) | Expert's time agreed? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |

- A source is a real job or a real question, not a topic. "How we cut a client's month-end close from six days to two" is a source. "Finance automation trends" is not.
- The expert is whoever did the work. They do not write; they talk and they check.
- Aim for six to ten sources. Mark the three with a willing expert and no permission problem. Those come first.

### Done when

- The list has at least three sources, each with an expert and a decision on what can be public.
- The person has confirmed the first three.

### Mark blocked when

- The experts have not agreed to give time. Give the person the list and the "twenty minutes" request to take to them.
- Nothing can be talked about without client permission that has not been asked for yet.

### Summary to report

State the number of sources and experts by role. Example: "Eight sources listed from real jobs and customer questions, across four experts. Three are ready: expert time agreed, two anonymised, one public."

## content.piece: Write a first piece from one real job

### Ask

To the expert, or to the person relaying for them. One at a time.

1. "What was the situation when you started? What was the client or the team struggling with?"
2. "What did you try first, and what did you actually end up doing?"
3. "What was harder than expected?"
4. "What changed afterwards? Give me only what you measured or saw yourself."
5. "What would you tell someone facing the same thing tomorrow?"
6. "Is there anything in this that must not be published?"

### Do

1. Take notes in the expert's words. Do not add facts, figures or quotes they did not give. Where something is missing, ask or leave it out.
2. Agree the format and length: for example an article of about 800 words, or a short post.
3. Draft the piece using the voice file. Structure: the problem as the reader would recognise it, what was done and why, what was hard, what changed, what the reader can take from it.
4. Anonymise the client unless permission is confirmed. Remove anything from question 6.
5. Show the draft with a list of every factual claim in it, each marked with where it came from in the notes. A claim with no source comes out.
6. The expert checks the facts. The person, or whoever owns the voice, checks how it sounds. Apply changes, at most two rounds.

### Done when

- A full draft exists, written from one real job in the expert's words.
- Every factual claim traces back to the notes, and the expert has confirmed the facts.
- The client is anonymised or their permission is recorded.

### Mark blocked when

- The expert is not available and the person cannot relay the detail.
- The piece cannot be made anonymous enough and permission has not been given.

### Summary to report

State the kind of piece and how it was checked. Do not include its text or the client. Example: "First article drafted from one real job, about 800 words, in the approved voice. Facts checked by the expert who did the work; client anonymised. Two rounds of edits."

## content.publish: Agree who checks it and how it gets published

### Ask

1. "Who checks facts before anything goes out, and who checks the voice?"
2. "Who gives the final yes?"
3. "Where does it get published, and who can publish there today?"
4. "How often can you realistically publish a piece like this?"
5. "Is there a developer or agency who looks after the website?"

### Do

Write the routine on half a page and show it.

```markdown
# Content routine

Rhythm: one piece every [period]. Sources: the source list, top to bottom.

## For each piece
1. Twenty minutes with the expert (role). Notes taken in their words.
2. Draft in Claude with the voice file.
3. Facts checked by: the expert. Voice checked by: [role]. Final yes: [role].
4. Published to [where] by [role].
5. Source list updated: done, next one picked.

## Rules
- Nothing is published without the expert's check.
- No client named without written permission.
- No figures that were not measured.
```

Then settle how publishing works, and be honest about the two levels.

- **By hand** (easy). Someone copies the approved piece into the website's editor and publishes it. This works today and is enough to start.
- **Straight from Claude to the website or its content system** (hard). This means a connector or automation that can create a draft in the content system, or a change in the website's code repository, with a person approving before it goes live. It saves the copy-and-paste step and keeps formatting consistent. It needs someone technical: the developer or agency who looks after the site. If the person wants it, write down what it should do (create a draft, never publish without approval, which fields to fill) so that person can build from it.

Start by hand. Record the direct route as a hard item if the person wants it.

### Done when

- The routine names who checks facts, who checks voice, who approves and who publishes.
- The first piece is published by hand, or has a date and a person to publish it.
- If direct publishing is wanted, it is written down as a hard item with what it should do.

### Mark blocked when

- Nobody can publish to the website today, or the person does not know who can.
- The approver is not available to agree the routine.

### Summary to report

State the routine and the publishing route. Example: "Routine agreed: one piece a fortnight, facts checked by the expert, final yes from the marketing lead. Publishing by hand for now; direct publishing to the content system recorded as a hard item for the web developer."
