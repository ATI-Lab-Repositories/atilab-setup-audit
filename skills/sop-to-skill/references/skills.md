# Skills from your experts

_Generated from the course source. Do not edit this file by hand._

Outcome: One skill built from a senior person's own method, an owner for it and a ranked list of the next ones. About 50 minutes.

A skill is a small folder of written instructions that Claude loads when a matching task comes up. A plugin is a package that bundles several skills, and sometimes connectors, so a team gets them together.

The skills that last are the ones written from how the company's own best people do the work. In three teams we observed, more than 85% of the skills taken from online libraries were dropped, and the skills built from the team's own know-how were the ones used most and rated best. So this module does not go looking for skills to install. It lists what is there, finds the procedures that matter, builds one skill with a senior person's real example, and sets up who looks after skills from now on.

Building a skill is easy. Keeping many skills organised for several teams is harder, and the last step says where that starts.

## skills.inventory: List the skills and plugins you have, where each came from and which ones clash

### Ask

1. "Here are the skills and plugins I can see in this session. Is anything missing?" If you cannot see them: "Open your skills list and read me the names. Then the plugins."
2. For each: "Where did it come from: built here, taken from an online library, part of a plugin, or provided by Anthropic?"
3. "Which of these did someone use in the last month?"
4. "Do any two of them do the same job?"

### Do

Start from your own list of skills and plugins in this session, if you can see it. Do not search past chats or memory. Build the table.

| Skill or plugin | Came from | Built or chosen by (role) | Used last month? | Overlaps with | Decision |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |

Look for these and say what you find:

- **Clashes.** A standalone skill that does the same job as a skill inside a plugin, or two skills with near-identical descriptions. Claude picks a skill from its description, so with two alike it may load either one, and results vary.
- **Unused imports.** Skills taken from online libraries that nobody has used. They still take up room in the list Claude chooses from.
- **No owner.** Skills nobody can say who wrote or who would fix.
- **No plugins at all.** Fine with a handful of skills. With many skills across several teams it means there is no way to give each team its own set.

Mark each **keep**, **remove** or **merge with [skill]**. The person turns off or removes what they agree to. For skills the company provides to everyone, only an owner can remove them; list those for the owner.

### Done when

- Every skill and plugin the person can see has a source and a decision.
- Clashes are named, or the table says "none found".
- Agreed removals are done or listed with who will do them.

### Mark blocked when

- The person cannot see the organisation's skills and plugins and the owner is not available. Record what they can see and give them the table headings.

### Summary to report

State the counts by source and what was decided. Example: "23 skills and 2 plugins listed: 5 built here, 16 from online libraries, 2 from plugins. 14 unused and removed. Two clashes found between standalone skills and plugin skills."

## skills.sops: List the core procedures by team and the senior person who knows each best

### Ask

A procedure here means a piece of work the team does again and again in a similar way. One team at a time.

1. "Which teams should we cover? Start with the two or three where most of the repeated work is."
2. For each team: "What are the procedures this team runs every week or month? Reports, replies, documents from a template, checks, handovers."
3. For each procedure: "Who is the senior person who does it best? Role only."
4. "How often does it run, how many people do it, and how long does it take each time?"
5. "Is the method written down anywhere, or is it in that person's head?"
6. "Does a person check the result before it goes anywhere?"

### Do

Build the list and show it after each team.

| Team | Procedure | Best senior person (role) | Runs per week | Minutes per run | People doing it | Written down? | Checked before use? | Hours per week |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |

- Hours per week = runs per week × minutes per run × people doing it ÷ 60. Rough numbers are fine.
- Five to ten procedures per team is enough. Leave out one-off projects.
- A procedure with no senior person who can explain it is not ready to become a skill. Mark it "method not agreed".
- Sort by hours per week. This is the starting backlog.

### Done when

- At least one team has its procedures listed with a senior person, frequency and time for each.
- The person has confirmed the list.

### Mark blocked when

- The person does not know a team's procedures and nobody from that team is here. Give them the table headings to take to the team lead.

### Summary to report

State how many procedures were listed and the rough total time. Example: "Listed 14 procedures across two teams, about 30 hours a week in total. Nine have a senior person who can explain the method; five are not agreed yet."

## skills.build: Turn one procedure into a working skill and test it on a second example

This step is repeatable. Run it again for each procedure on the list, one at a time.

### Ask

First choose the procedure:

- "I suggest we build [procedure]: it has the most hours among the ones that are checked by a person and have a senior person to explain them. Do you agree?"
- "Can the senior person join for twenty minutes, or have they given you two real past examples with names removed?"

Then, to the senior person or the person relaying for them:

1. "Share one real example: what you started from and the finished version. Remove names and anything confidential."
2. "Talk me through how you got from one to the other. What do you always do first?"
3. "What does a good one always include? What must it never include?"
4. "What do people get wrong when they are new to this?"
5. "When should Claude stop and ask instead of carrying on?"
6. "What would you naturally type to ask for this?"
7. "How long does one run take you today?"

After the test: "Compare this with what you actually produced. What is different? Would you use it after light edits? How long would this run take now?"

### Do

**Choose.** Take the procedure with the most hours where all of these are true: a person reads the result before it is used; it does not move money or make a commitment on its own; it does not need sensitive personal data pasted in; a senior person can explain the method; two real examples exist. Do not pick a riskier procedure because it has more hours.

**Write the skill** from the senior person's method, not from general knowledge. A skill is a folder containing a file named `SKILL.md`, which starts with two fields.

- `name`: lowercase letters, numbers and hyphens, 64 characters at most, and it must not contain "claude" or "anthropic". The folder has the same name.
- `description`: what the skill does and when to use it, with the words the person would type (question 6). Keep it to 200 characters so the Claude app accepts the upload.
- Body: instructions to Claude, in order, in plain words. Describe the method, not the one example. Say what to do when something is missing: ask, do not guess. End with checks Claude runs before showing the result.
- Put long material (a full template, the worked example) in separate files in a `references` folder and mention each one in `SKILL.md`.
- No names, client details, credentials or personal data anywhere in the skill. Everyone who gets the skill gets its files.

```markdown
---
name: [procedure-name-in-lowercase-with-hyphens]
description: [What it produces], from [what it starts from]. Use when the user asks for [the words they would type].
---

# [Procedure name]

## What this is for
[One or two sentences: the work, who the result is for, what happens to it next.]

## What you need before you start
- [Input 1]
- [Input 2]
If any of these is missing, ask for it. Do not invent content.

## Steps
1. [First thing to do with the input.]
2. [Next step.]
3. Write the result in the format below.
4. Run the checks before you show the result.

## Output format
[Sections in order, length, tone. For a fixed layout: "Follow references/template.md exactly."]

## Rules
- Always [rule, with the reason].
- Never [rule, with the reason].
- If [situation], stop and ask the user.

## Checks before you show the result
- [ ] Every figure and date comes from the input, not from memory.
- [ ] The format matches the template.
- [ ] [A check specific to this procedure.]
- [ ] The result is marked as a draft for a person to review.

## Reference files
- `references/template.md`: the layout to follow.
- `references/example.md`: one worked example with details removed. Use it to judge tone and detail, not as content to copy.
```

Show the full `SKILL.md` and each reference file. Apply the senior person's changes.

**Produce the files yourself.** If you can create files in this session, make the folder, then a zip with the folder itself at the top level, and give the person the zip. All they do is upload it: in the Claude app, under **Customize > Skills**, choose **+**, then **Create skill**, then **Upload a skill**, and switch it on. Skills need code execution and file creation to be turned on. In Claude Code, save the folder under `.claude/skills/` in the project or `~/.claude/skills/` for one person; write the files yourself if the person agrees.

**Fallback, if you cannot create files.** Give the person the content of each file in a code block, with its file name. They create a folder named exactly as the skill, save `SKILL.md` inside it, save the reference files in a `references` folder inside that, compress the folder into a zip so the folder is the top level, and upload the zip as above.

**Test on a second example, now.** In a new chat, the person asks for the work in their own words without naming the skill, and gives only the starting material from the second example.

1. If the skill does not load, the description is the problem. Rewrite it with the person's words and try again.
2. Compare the result with the senior person's own finished version. Sort each difference: the skill is wrong; a matter of style; or the skill is better.
3. Fix what is wrong, show the change, get approval, produce the new zip and run the same example again. Stop after two rounds.

**Record the time.** Write down minutes per run before and after, as the senior person estimates them, and multiply by runs per week. This is the first figure for "hours returned each week on named tasks". Say that it is an estimate until the skill has run for a few weeks.

### Done when

- `SKILL.md` and its reference files exist and the senior person, or the person on their behalf, has approved them.
- The skill is installed in at least one place and loaded from a natural request.
- On the second example, the result would be used after light edits.
- Time per run before and after is recorded.

### Mark blocked when

- No senior person can explain the method, or the people who do the work disagree about it. Someone has to settle the method first.
- No real example can be shared, even with details removed.
- Skills or code execution are turned off for the organisation and the person is not an owner. Give them the zip and say who can turn it on.
- The result is still not usable after two rounds of fixes. Say what kind of difference remains.

### Summary to report

State what kind of procedure the skill covers, the test result and the time estimate. Do not include its text. Example: "Built a skill for the weekly status report from the team lead's method. Passed on a second example after one fix. Estimated time per run fell from 40 minutes to 10, about two hours a week returned."

## skills.ownership: Name the owner, the update route and the next skills to build

### Ask

1. "For each team: which senior person owns that team's skills? They decide what the skill says and fix it when it gives a bad result."
2. "How do colleagues report a bad result?"
3. "Who will publish updates: each owner, or one person for the whole company?"
4. "Does the company use GitHub, and is there someone comfortable with it?"
5. "Looking at the list of procedures: which three should become skills next?"

### Do

**Owners.** One senior person per team owns that team's skills. Record roles, not names. A skill without an owner goes stale and people stop trusting it.

**Update route.** Skills drift when copies are passed around and edited separately. Pick one route so everyone always has the same version. Check the organisation pages listed at the top of this module before you describe the steps; they change.

- **One maintainer.** One person, often an executive or the champion, keeps the master copy of every skill and is the only one who publishes. On Team and Enterprise plans an owner can provide a skill to everyone from the organisation settings page for plugins and skills, and a person can submit their own skill with **Publish to org**; an owner can require review of each submission. This suits a small number of skills. It is easy.
- **A plugin marketplace on GitHub.** A marketplace here is a GitHub repository that lists the company's plugins, with one plugin per team holding that team's skills. An owner connects the repository once under the organisation's plugin settings, on the **Marketplaces** tab. When this module was written, the repository had to be private or internal, an update reached members after a change with a new plugin version was merged, and each plugin could be set as installed for everyone, available to install, required or hidden. Each team's owner proposes changes; one person approves them. This suits many skills across several teams. Setting up the repository and the plugins is a technical job.

Say which route fits now. One maintainer is the right start for most companies. Move to the marketplace when there are more skills than one person can keep in their head, or when teams need different sets.

**Backlog.** Take the list from `skills.sops`, remove what is built, and rank the next three by hours per week, with the senior person for each.

Write it on one page and show it.

```markdown
# Skills register

Update route: [one maintainer: role] / [plugin marketplace: repository owner role]. Bad results are reported to: [channel].

| Skill | Team | Owner (role) | Built from | Last reviewed |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |

## Next to build
1. [Procedure], [team], with [senior role]. About [hours] a week.
2. [Procedure], [team], with [senior role]. About [hours] a week.
3. [Procedure], [team], with [senior role]. About [hours] a week.
```

**Name the hard part.** Building the next skills is easy: repeat `skills.build`. Organising many skills into a plugin for each team, kept up to date from one place, is the hard part. It needs someone technical: a person comfortable with GitHub and with how plugins are packaged. Write that line into the register so it is not forgotten, and mark it hard.

### Done when

- Every existing skill that is kept has an owner by role.
- One update route is chosen and written down, with who publishes.
- The next three skills are ranked, each with a senior person.

### Mark blocked when

- No senior person in a team will take ownership. Record which team.
- The person is not an owner and cannot provide skills to the organisation or open the plugin settings. Give them the register to pass on.

Choosing the marketplace route without anyone technical to set it up is not a blocker for this step. Record the route as "one maintainer for now" and the marketplace as a hard item still to do.

### Summary to report

State the route, how many skills have owners and what is next. Example: "Three team owners named for seven skills. Update route: one maintainer for now; a plugin marketplace recorded as a hard item. Next three procedures ranked, about nine hours a week between them."
